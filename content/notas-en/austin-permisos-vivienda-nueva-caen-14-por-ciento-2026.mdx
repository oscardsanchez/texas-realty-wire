The Austin metro logged just over 15,000 new-home construction permits between January and August 2026 — 14% fewer than in the same period of 2025, according to Austin Business Journal data cited by The Real Deal. The decline is concentrated almost entirely in apartments: multifamily permits fell 36% year over year and 79% from five years ago, while single-family permits slipped only 2% year over year (38% over five years).

## Context

The multifamily slump is not new, but it is deepening: Austin has spent several years delivering the apartment surplus built during the 2021–2023 boom, and developers keep holding back new projects while those units finish leasing up and absorption normalizes. On top of that come higher construction costs and more expensive financing conditions for multifamily, which make starting a new project harder to justify than it was two or three years ago.

Single-family, by contrast, is holding up relatively well — a drop of just 2% year over year suggests that homebuilders still see enough demand to keep their permit pace, even with elevated mortgage rates and a slower resale market. According to The Real Deal, Austin and San Antonio recorded the steepest annual permit declines among the large Sun Belt metros, both above 24%.

## What to do with this

- If you work with multifamily investors, keep in mind that new apartment supply will keep contracting through 2027–2028 — it is the window to position acquisitions of existing properties before absorption tightens rents again.
- For builder or lot-developer clients, the slowdown in multifamily permits can translate into apartment-zoned land available at better prices — it is worth tracking who is selling land positions they no longer plan to develop in the short term.
- If you sell new homes, the fact that single-family permits barely fell is an argument for keeping buyers looking at builder inventory: new supply is still arriving at a pace close to 2025's, despite the market's overall cooling.
- Monitor the next permit releases from the City of Austin and the metro area — if the 14% decline deepens in the final months of 2026, it is a sign that the adjustment has not yet bottomed out.
